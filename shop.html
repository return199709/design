The eleven dried roots, seeds and berries that go into the pot. Each one is written up in full in the Journal.

Chinese angelica Warm, sweet and faintly medicinal. The one people reach for when the body runs cold. 3 g in the pot Lotus seed Quietly sweet, soft after a long simmer. Used to settle a restless heart. 20 g in the pot Lily bulb Soft and a little sweet. An autumn one, for a dry cough and a busy mind. 10 g in the pot Lilyturf root Tiny amber tubers. Everything about this one is moisture: a dry throat, a thirst water does not fix. 10 g in the pot Pseudostellaria root Prince ginseng. The gentle way to build energy back up, mild enough for children. 5 g in the pot Astragalus root Sweet and woody in broth. The workhorse for low energy, and the most common tonic in a home kitchen. 5 g, or steeped like tea Fritillaria bulb The expensive one, gathered by hand high in the mountains. Known above all for a dry cough. 3 g, steamed in a pear Goji berry Sweet, a bit like a raisin with a tomato edge. Best known for the eyes, and the easiest one to use daily. 5 g, in soup or hot water Fragrant Solomon's seal Faintly sweet, a little sticky once cooked. Adds moisture without sitting heavy. 10 g in the pot Job's tears Chewy and nutty. This is the one for damp — but it is cooling, so go easy if you already run cold. 20 g in the pot Gordon euryale seed Floury and soft after a long simmer, enough to thicken a soup. Almost always cooked alongside lotus seed. 10 g in the pot

Quantities are the amounts used in a household pot, not a dose. These are traditional uses, not medical advice.
